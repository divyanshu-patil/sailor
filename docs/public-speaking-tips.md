# Public Speaking Tips

68 short, practical tips from well-known speakers and coaches, grouped by when you use them. Each one is a single thing to do, and says who it comes from.

---

## Before you speak

1. **Know your one idea.** If you can't say it in one sentence, the audience won't remember it. — *Chris Anderson (TED)*
2. **Decide what they should know or do at the end.** It's your speech's GPS: every line should move toward it. — *MicFront*
3. **Make the audience the hero.** You're the guide; they're the ones who change. — *Nancy Duarte*
4. **Give them a reason to care.** Start from a question or problem they already have. — *Chris Anderson*
5. **Explain the new with the familiar.** Use their words, analogies and examples. — *Chris Anderson*
6. **Write it simple.** Short sentences, short words, no jargon. If you'd never say it out loud, cut it. — *MicFront, Carmine Gallo*
7. **Group it in threes.** Three points are easy to follow and easy to remember. — *Carmine Gallo*
8. **Keep it short.** Aim for under 18 minutes; less content gets remembered more. — *Carmine Gallo*
9. **Plan one moment they'll retell.** A prop, a stat, a demo. Bill Gates released mosquitoes at TED. — *Carmine Gallo*
10. **Make the numbers human.** One real person's story moves a room more than any statistic. — *Simon Sinek*
11. **Don't let slides flatten your story.** Tell it as a person, with your own stakes in it; a slide on its own has no pulse. — *Nancy Duarte*
12. **Start with why.** Lead with why it matters, then what it is. — *Simon Sinek*
13. **Talk about an idea, not your company.** A talk about an organisation feels distant, and easily reads as a sales pitch. — *Chris Anderson*
14. **Write the talk before the slides.** Slides come after the speech; in a small room, you may not need any. — *Nancy Duarte*
15. **Don't wing it.** Underprepared talks ramble. You owe the room a connected talk. — *Chris Anderson*

## Opening

16. **Walk on, breathe, then speak.** Don't talk while walking out. Find your spot, pause, begin. — *Simon Sinek*
17. **Open with a promise.** Tell them what they'll know at the end that they don't know now. — *Patrick Winston (MIT)*
18. **Open with a hook, not a joke.** Use a story, a question or a striking fact; save jokes until they know you. — *MicFront, Patrick Winston*
19. **Take 15 seconds to introduce yourself.** When everyone gives a name and a job, add a short story. That's what they'll remember. — *Vinh Giang*

## Your voice

20. **Play your voice like an instrument.** Vary your speed, volume, pitch and pauses; monotone loses rooms. — *Vinh Giang (@askvinh)*
21. **Slow down for what matters.** Move through context quickly, then slow right down on the key line. — *Vinh Giang*
22. **End sentences on a lower pitch.** A downward finish sounds certain; a rising one sounds like a question. — *Vinh Giang, Vanessa Van Edwards*
23. **Slow and lower your pace overall.** It reads as calm, grounded and in control. — *Jefferson Fisher*
24. **Pause instead of "um".** Silence replaces filler words and makes you look sure. — *Matt Abrahams, Vinh Giang*
25. **Pause after your big lines.** Give people time to take it in; silence also pulls attention back. — *Vinh Giang, MicFront*
26. **Warm up your voice.** Big sigh, lip trills ("brrr"), "ba-ba-ba", "la-la-la" before you go on. — *Julian Treasure*
27. **Speak with HAIL.** Honesty, authenticity, integrity, love: people listen to people they trust. — *Julian Treasure*
28. **Turn up the volume.** Volume carries everything else in your voice; without it the rest is lost. — *Vinh Giang*
29. **Slow down to sound clearer.** Especially with an accent: slowing gives your mouth time to shape every word. — *Vinh Giang*
30. **Stretch your range.** Read a page aloud with exaggerated intonation. It builds range like lifting weights builds strength. — *Julian Treasure*
31. **Say it like "pass the salt".** Plain and certain. Take the doubt out of your tone. — *Jefferson Fisher*

## Your body

32. **One person, one thought.** Hold one person's eyes for a full sentence, then move on. Don't scan the room. — *Simon Sinek, Vinh Giang*
33. **Use your hands.** The most-viewed TED speakers used almost twice as many gestures as the least-viewed. — *Vanessa Van Edwards*
34. **Smile when you can.** More smiling tracked with more views, even on serious topics. — *Vanessa Van Edwards*
35. **Stand tall, move with purpose.** Good posture signals confidence; walk to make a point, not to pace. — *MicFront, Matt Abrahams*
36. **Keep your hands visible.** People trust what they can see; hidden hands make you harder to read. — *Vanessa Van Edwards*
37. **Move your face to move them.** Your face puts the emotion into your voice; a still face makes excited words sound flat. — *Vinh Giang*
38. **Let your face match your words.** People read your face for context, and a mismatch feels fake. — *Vinh Giang*
39. **Mirror the room's energy.** Big with big, quiet with quiet. — *Vinh Giang*
40. **Steeple your hands when still.** Fingertips touching, palms apart: rated the most charismatic resting gesture. — *Vanessa Van Edwards*
41. **Nod slowly, three times.** When someone asks a question, a slow triple nod says "I'm listening"; a fast one says "hurry up". — *Vanessa Van Edwards*

## While you speak

42. **Use What → So what → Now what.** The thing, why it matters, the next step. It works for any answer. — *Matt Abrahams*
43. **Say your key idea three times.** People drift, and repeating it brings them back. — *Patrick Winston*
44. **Signpost with numbers and questions.** "Point two…" lets anyone who drifted catch back up. — *Patrick Winston*
45. **Tell a story, not a list.** Stories are what people remember and repeat. — *MicFront*
46. **Contrast what is with what could be.** Go back and forth to build tension, then show the way forward. — *Nancy Duarte*
47. **Breathe before you answer.** A beat before replying shows you heard the question; a rapid-fire answer sounds unsure. — *Jefferson Fisher*
48. **Drop "I think".** "I'm confident that…" lands; hedges shrink your point before you've made it. — *Jefferson Fisher*
49. **Notice when you've said enough.** Circling your point, losing the question, or glazed faces all mean: wrap it up. — *Jefferson Fisher*
50. **Believe every word.** No trick persuades like conviction. If you believe it, they will. — *Vinh Giang*
51. **Put on the spot? Use 3-2-1.** Answer with three steps, two types, or the one thing. A frame beats rambling. — *Vinh Giang*
52. **Drop "does that make sense?"** It sounds unsure. Ask "What are your thoughts?" instead. — *Jefferson Fisher*
53. **Don't trail off with "so".** Finish the sentence and stop. — *Jefferson Fisher*
54. **Lead with your ask.** Say what you need first; give the reasons when they ask. — *Jefferson Fisher*
55. **Cut the speed bumps.** "I was wondering if… it's not a big deal, but…" Just say it plainly. — *Jefferson Fisher*
56. **Say it in three sentences.** If you can't, rephrase it. Authority doesn't over-explain. — *Jefferson Fisher*

## Nerves

57. **Say "I'm excited", not "calm down".** People who reframed nerves as excitement gave more persuasive, confident speeches. — *Alison Wood Brooks (Harvard), Simon Sinek*
58. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Slow exhales calm your body fastest. — *Matt Abrahams*
59. **Notice the nerves, don't fight them.** Anxiety is normal. Name it and let it sit beside you. — *Matt Abrahams*
60. **Loosen up with a tongue twister.** It warms your mouth and shakes off the tension. — *Matt Abrahams*
61. **Serve them, don't perform.** Put your focus on what the audience needs, not on how you look. — *Vinh Giang, Chris Anderson*
62. **Act first, confidence follows.** Don't wait to feel confident. Saying the assertive thing is what creates the feeling. — *Jefferson Fisher*
63. **Train courage like a muscle.** Speak more often; what scares you shrinks once it's routine. — *Vinh Giang*

## Closing

64. **End on a line they'll remember.** Restate your key message in one strong sentence. — *MicFront*
65. **Don't end on "thank you" or "any questions?"** End on what they now have. That's your last word. — *Patrick Winston*

## Practice

66. **Record and review.** Film a 5-minute unscripted talk. Watch it muted (body), listen without the picture (voice), then transcribe it (filler words). Practise until it's muscle memory. — *Vinh Giang, MicFront*
67. **Rehearse in front of one or two people.** A little pressure in practice makes the real thing feel familiar. — *Carmine Gallo, Sian Beilock*
68. **Know it by heart.** Rehearse until your mouth knows the next line, even if your mind goes blank. — *Guy Winch, via Carmine Gallo*
